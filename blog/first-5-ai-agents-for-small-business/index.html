The First 5 AI Agents Every Small Business Should Hire

Answer every call. Reply to every lead in minutes. Get your evenings back, one narrow agent at a time.

If you could hire five new people tomorrow, you would not hire five salespeople. You would hire someone to answer the phone, someone to call back every new lead right away, someone to ask happy customers for reviews, someone to clean up the inbox, and someone to tell you each morning what actually matters today. That is the same order we recommend for AI agents for small business, and this guide walks through each one hour by hour.

Most owners who look into AI agents for business get stuck at the same spot: the list of what is possible is endless, and nobody tells them where to start. The five agents below tend to give the fastest time back to the widest range of small businesses. Each does one narrow job well, connects to tools you probably already use, and has clear points where a human stays in charge. If you are new to the idea, our primer on what an AI agent army actually is is a good five-minute warm-up.

The short version: Receptionist first, then Speed-to-Lead, then Reviews, then Inbox Triage, then a Daily Briefing. Buy one, buy two, or buy all five. Starting small is normal, and many owners do.

The lineup

The first five AI agents, in the order we recommend

1. AI Receptionist

Answers calls and after-hours inquiries, captures details, books what it is allowed to book, and texts you a summary.

2. Speed-to-Lead + Follow-Up

Replies to new web and ad leads in minutes, then keeps following up politely until someone answers or opts out.

3. Review Request Agent

Asks every finished customer for an honest review, at the right time, through the right channel.

4. Inbox Triage Agent

Sorts, labels and drafts so you only open the email that needs you.

5. Daily Briefing Agent

One short morning report: calls, leads, reviews, money in, money out, and what needs a decision.

Later: a Commander

When you run five or more agents, a Commander routes work between them and handles approvals in one place.

Why this order

Why response speed sits at the top of the list

The first two agents on this list exist for the same reason: in most small businesses, the money leaks out between the moment a customer reaches out and the moment someone gets back to them. That gap is not a guess. It has been measured.

In a study published in Harvard Business Review, researchers James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran and David Elkington audited how 2,241 US companies responded to web leads ("The Short Life of Online Sales Leads," HBR, 2011). As summarized by SmartCompany, only 37% responded within an hour, 23% never responded at all, and the average response time among companies that did reply within 30 days was 42 hours. Firms that contacted a lead within an hour were nearly 7 times as likely to qualify it (have a meaningful conversation with a decision maker) as firms that waited even one more hour, and more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited 24 hours or longer.

That research is more than a decade old, but the logic holds. A person who just filled out your form is thinking about the problem right now. An hour later they are on another site, or talking to whoever picked up first.

A pair of narrow agents can now cover that gap around the clock, with a person still owning the conversation once it gets real. That is why the phone-answering squad and the speed-to-lead agent come first.

Agent 1 · Specialist

1. The AI receptionist and after-hours answering agent

An AI receptionist is a voice and text agent that picks up when your team cannot. It is not a phone tree. It holds a natural conversation, answers the questions you have approved, collects the details your team needs, and either books the appointment or hands the call to a person.

What it does hour to hour

During business hours it typically works as overflow: if the front desk is on another line or the owner is on a job site, the call rolls to the agent instead of voicemail. After hours, on weekends and on holidays, it becomes the first voice a caller hears. A typical call looks like this: it greets the caller with your business name, asks what they need, answers common questions (hours, service area, what you do and do not do), collects name, number, address and the details of the job, checks your calendar for open slots, and offers times. When the call ends, you get a text or email summary with the caller's details and a short note on what they want.

Tools it connects to

A phone number or call-forwarding setup from your current carrier or VoIP provider, your calendar (Google Calendar, Outlook or your scheduling software), your CRM or customer list (HubSpot, a field-service platform, or even a shared spreadsheet), and SMS for summaries and confirmations.

What stays human

Pricing commitments, emergency judgment calls, complaints and anything legally sensitive. You set the rules, such as booking free estimates but not paid service calls. Callers who ask for a person can always reach one or leave a message. Our guardrails and human-approval approach covers how those lines get drawn.

What can go wrong, and how to guard against it

  • It answers something it should not. Guard: a written answer list, a "do not discuss" list, and a default of "let me have someone call you back" for anything outside it.
  • It double-books. Guard: it reads live calendar availability and can be held to offering slots, not confirming them, until your team approves.
  • A real emergency waits until morning. Guard: keyword and intent rules (flooding, no heat, gas smell, injury) that trigger an immediate transfer or text to the on-call person.
  • Callers do not know they are talking to AI. Guard: we recommend the agent says so plainly, and recording or disclosure rules in your state are checked during setup.

Setup prerequisites

Your top caller questions and approved answers, service area, booking rules, an on-call contact, and calendar access.

Agent 2 · Specialist

2. The speed-to-lead responder and follow-up agent: your first AI sales agent

The receptionist handles people who call. This agent handles everyone else: website form fills, quote requests, ad leads from Google or Facebook, marketplace inquiries, and chat messages. Think of it as an AI sales agent with one narrow assignment: make sure every lead hears from you within minutes, and nobody falls through the cracks after the first message.

What it does hour to hour

A new lead lands. Within a minute or two, the agent sends a personal-sounding text and email that references what the person actually asked for, confirms the best way to reach them, and offers a time to talk or a link to book. If your team has someone available, it can alert them to call right now while the lead is warm. If the lead does not reply, the follow-up side takes over: a short, polite sequence over the next several days, spaced out and written in your voice, that stops the moment the person replies, books, or says no thanks. Every touch gets logged in your CRM.

Tools it connects to

Your website forms, ad platforms' lead forms, your CRM (HubSpot, a field-service system, a real estate CRM), SMS and email sending, and your calendar or booking link.

What stays human

The actual sales conversation, pricing, discounts and commitments. The agent's job is to start the conversation and keep it from going cold, not to close. If a lead asks for a price, the agent can share published starting prices you approve, or set the expectation that a person will follow up with a real quote. Quote drafting is a separate agent you can add later.

What can go wrong, and how to guard against it

  • It feels pushy. Guard: a short sequence with real spacing, a clear opt-out in every message, and a hard stop on any reply.
  • Texting rules. Guard: consent capture on your forms and opt-out handling are set up before the first text goes out. Rules vary by country and state, so this is checked during the Blueprint.
  • Duplicate outreach. Guard: the agent checks the CRM before messaging so an existing customer does not get a "nice to meet you" text.
  • Wrong tone for your market. Guard: you approve every template before it goes live, and you can review the first batch of real messages.

Setup prerequisites

A list of every place leads come from, CRM access, a sending number and domain set up for SMS and email, your approved message templates, and a decision on who gets the "call now" alert.

Tier and starting price

The Speed-to-Lead Responder and the Follow-Up Agent are both Specialists: from $3,500 each to build and own, or from $299/mo + setup each on Subscribe-to-Own. Many owners start with the responder alone and add follow-up once they see how many leads go quiet after the first message.

Agent 3 · Scout

3. The review request agent

Every owner knows reviews matter, and almost every owner forgets to ask, because the ask has to happen right as the job wraps up and everyone is moving on.

The research backs up both halves of the problem. In BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey 2025, 96% of consumers said they are open to writing a review for a business, yet only 29% said they actually wrote one in the past year. The same survey found email was the most effective way to ask, rising from 32% to 40%, with in-person requests second at 27%. People are willing; they just need to be asked.

What it does hour to hour

It watches for a "job complete" signal: an invoice marked paid, an appointment marked done, an order delivered. After a delay you choose (same day for a restaurant visit, a day or two for a service call), it sends a short, friendly request by email or text with a direct link to your Google Business Profile or another review site. If there is no response, it sends one gentle reminder and then stops. It keeps a log of who was asked and when, so nobody is asked twice.

Tools it connects to

Your invoicing or job system (QuickBooks, a field-service platform, a booking tool or your ecommerce store), email and SMS, and your review profile links.

What stays human

Anything involving an unhappy customer. If a job was flagged with a problem, the agent skips it and routes it to you. Replying to reviews, especially negative ones, also stays with a person, or goes through a separate Review Reply Agent that drafts responses for your approval.

What can go wrong, and how to guard against it

  • Breaking review rules. In the US, the FTC's rule on consumer reviews, effective October 2024, bars incentives conditioned on a positive or negative review and bars suppressing negative reviews, as Davis Wright Tremaine summarized. Guard: the agent asks everyone for an honest review, offers no sentiment-based reward, and never filters who can post publicly.
  • Asking at a bad moment. Guard: it reads job status and skips anything flagged as a complaint, warranty call or open issue.
  • Annoying people. Guard: one request, one reminder, done.

Setup prerequisites

A reliable "job done" signal in your software, your direct review links, and approved message wording.

Tier and starting price

The Review Request Agent is a Scout: from $1,500 to build and own, or from $149/mo plus setup on Subscribe-to-Own. It is one of the easiest agents on this list to deploy.

Agent 4 · Scout

4. The inbox triage agent

Once calls and leads are covered, the next drain for most owners is the inbox. Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index found employees are interrupted every two minutes during the workday, about 275 interruptions a day across meetings, email and chat. An inbox agent will not fix all of that, but it can take the email part down to the messages that need you.

What it does hour to hour

Every new email gets read and sorted: customer, vendor, invoice, lead, scheduling, newsletter, spam-ish. It applies labels, pulls out anything urgent, forwards leads to the speed-to-lead agent, files receipts for bookkeeping, and drafts replies to routine messages for you to review and send. At set times (say 11 a.m. and 4 p.m.) it can send you a short "here is what needs you" digest so you are not living in your inbox.

Tools it connects to

Gmail or Microsoft 365/Outlook, your CRM for lead routing, and optionally your accounting tool or a shared folder for invoices and receipts.

What stays human

Sending. By default, the inbox agent drafts and you send. Letting it auto-send a narrow set of routine replies later is your call. It does not delete email by default, either; it labels and archives so nothing is lost.

What can go wrong, and how to guard against it

  • It mislabels something important. Guard: a "when in doubt, flag it" rule, a weekly review of anything it was unsure about, and tuning based on your corrections.
  • Access to sensitive email. Guard: scoped permissions, a clear list of what it may read, and data handling agreed before setup.
  • A draft sounds wrong. Guard: drafts only, until you say otherwise.

Setup prerequisites

Mailbox access with the right permissions, your sorting categories, a list of VIP senders, and a few examples of how you like to reply.

Tier and starting price

The Inbox Triage Agent is a Scout: from $1,500 to build and own, or from $149/mo plus setup on Subscribe-to-Own. If your email load is heavy, it is worth running your numbers through the Time-Back Calculator to see what it could free up.

Agent 5 · Scout

5. The daily briefing agent

Once four agents are working, a lot happens without you. The Daily Briefing Agent tells you what happened in one short morning report.

What it does hour to hour

Early each morning it pulls yesterday's numbers from the systems you choose and writes a plain-English summary: calls answered and booked, new leads and how fast they were contacted, reviews requested and received, money collected, invoices overdue, today's schedule, and a short list of items waiting on your decision. It arrives by email or text. If something unusual happened (a spike in missed calls, a one-star review, a big invoice past due), it moves that to the top.

Tools it connects to

Whatever holds your numbers: phone system, CRM, review profiles, accounting (QuickBooks or similar), calendar, and the logs from the other agents.

What stays human

Everything it reports on. The briefing agent reads and summarizes; it does not take action. That makes it one of the safest agents to run.

What can go wrong, and how to guard against it

  • Wrong numbers. Guard: it shows where each figure came from, so a quick spot-check is easy, and it says "no data" instead of guessing when a source is down.
  • Report fatigue. Guard: keep it short. If you stop reading it, we cut it down until you do.

Setup prerequisites

Read-only access to the tools it reports on, and a short list of the five to ten numbers you actually care about.

Tier and starting price

The Daily Briefing Agent is a Scout: from $1,500 to build and own, or from $149/mo plus setup on Subscribe-to-Own. It sits last because it gets more useful with every agent added before it.

Putting it together

How the five combine into your first squad

Picture a week. A homeowner calls at 8:40 p.m.; the AI receptionist answers and books an estimate. Someone else fills out your web form at 9:15 p.m.; the speed-to-lead agent texts them within minutes, and the follow-up agent checks in the next afternoon. Your team wins and finishes the job and marks the invoice paid; the review agent sends a thank-you and a review link. The inbox agent files supplier invoices and drafts a reply to a warranty question. Each morning, the briefing sums it up: calls booked, leads still quiet, new reviews, invoices overdue.

None of those agents set a price or spent a dollar. They did the repetitive parts so your people could do the parts that need judgment. If you want to see that rhythm play out across a full day, we wrote it up in a day in the life of an AI agent team.

Without the first five

  • After-hours calls go to voicemail
  • Web leads wait until someone has a free minute
  • Reviews get asked for when someone remembers
  • The inbox is a mix of urgent and junk
  • You find out what happened by digging through five apps

With the first five

  • Calls answered, details captured, summaries sent
  • Every lead hears back in minutes, then gets followed up
  • Every finished customer is asked once, honestly
  • Email sorted, drafts ready, urgent items flagged
  • One morning briefing with the decisions that need you

How to roll them out in waves

You can order one agent or hundreds, but we deploy in waves, starting with the ones that free up the most time fastest:

  1. Blueprint. A $750 Agent Blueprint maps your calls, leads, tools and approval rules into a written plan. It is credited toward the build if you move forward within 60 days.
  2. Wave one: answer faster. AI Receptionist (or the lighter after-hours agent) plus Speed-to-Lead. Watch real conversations, tune the answers.
  3. Wave two: quick wins. Review requests and inbox triage. Both are Scouts with simple tool connections.
  4. Wave three: visibility. The Daily Briefing Agent, once the others are generating data worth summarizing.
  5. Tune and expand. Add follow-up, quote drafting, invoice reminders or scheduling based on what the briefing shows you is still slow.

You also choose how hands-on you want to be: Done-For-You (we build, deploy and run it with Care), Co-Pilot (we build, your team deploys with our guidance), or Handoff (we deliver the configured agent with documentation). The full process is laid out on how the build works.

One caution: Gartner has predicted over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 over costs, unclear value or weak risk controls (Outlook Business). Five narrow agents, each tied to a measurable job with a human approving anything risky, is the opposite of that kind of project.

When to add a Commander

A Commander Agent coordinates other agents. It routes work between them, keeps one approval queue for you instead of five, watches for conflicts (like the follow-up agent texting someone the receptionist just booked), and produces cross-agent reporting. It is a Commander tier build: from $7,500 to build and own, or from $599/mo + setup on Subscribe-to-Own.

You probably do not need one on day one. Signs it is time:

  • You are running five or more agents and approvals are arriving from several directions.
  • Agents need to share context in real time (for example, booking, dispatch and follow-up all touching the same customer).
  • You have more than one location or team, and work needs routing by territory or skill.
  • Your daily briefing has grown into something you want to act on, not just read.

If you are at five agents or more, squad pricing also starts to help: agents share tools, data and setup, so the Army Builder shows estimated volume pricing of 10% off the build at five to nine agents. FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What is the best first AI agent for a small business?

For most service businesses, an AI receptionist or after-hours answering agent comes first, because missed calls and slow replies are where leads tend to leak out. A speed-to-lead responder for web and ad leads is usually the second agent.

How much does an AI receptionist cost?

Our AI Receptionist is a Specialist-tier agent, starting from $3,500 to build and own or from $299 per month plus setup on Subscribe-to-Own. A lighter After-Hours Answering Agent starts from $1,500 or $149 per month plus setup. Deployment is scoped and priced in writing after the Agent Blueprint, and phone minutes and AI usage are billed at cost or run on your own accounts.

Will an AI sales agent close deals or quote prices on its own?

No. Our speed-to-lead and follow-up agents are designed to start the conversation fast and keep it from going cold. Pricing, discounts and commitments stay with a person unless you approve specific published prices for the agent to share.

Do I have to buy all five agents at once?

No. Buying one or two agents is common. We deploy in waves, starting with the agents that free up the most time fastest, and you can add more whenever it makes sense.

When do I need a Commander agent?

A Commander coordinates other agents, keeps one approval queue and handles cross-agent reporting. It usually makes sense once you run five or more agents, have several teams or locations, or need agents to share context in real time.

Is it legal to use an agent to ask customers for reviews?

Asking customers for honest reviews is generally fine. In the US, the FTC rule on consumer reviews bars incentives conditioned on positive or negative reviews and bars suppressing negative ones, so our Review Request Agent asks everyone for an honest review and offers no sentiment-based reward.
